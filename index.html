1 · System
Which system should I pick?

Deuteron: the only bound two-nucleon state (spin 1, isospin 0). np singlet: neutron and proton with opposite spins; unbound. Diproton and Dineutron: two identical nucleons, also unbound; the diproton adds Coulomb repulsion. Custom pair: any two particles. n + ortho/para H₂: the classic experiment that measured the spin dependence of the np force.

What do these controls mean?

V₀ sets how deep (strong) the attraction is; R or a sets its range. λ multiplies the whole nuclear potential: λ = 1 is the model as published, λ = 1.2 is 20% stronger. Partial wave ℓ is the orbital angular momentum; ℓ > 0 adds a centrifugal barrier. Fit adjusts one parameter until the model reproduces the measured binding energy or scattering length.

3 · Numerics
Do I need to change these?

Usually not. h is the grid spacing; smaller is more accurate but slower. rmax is the size of the box the bound state must fit into; increase it for very weakly bound states. match r is where the scattering solution is joined to free waves; it must lie outside the force. Results that change when you halve h or double rmax are not yet converged.

Slow neutrons on ortho- and para-hydrogen

In H₂ the two proton spins couple to S = 0 (para) or S = 1 (ortho). A neutron with wavelength much longer than the 0.74 Å bond scatters coherently from both protons, so the cross sections depend on the triplet and singlet np scattering lengths separately, including their signs.

Cross sections

Schwinger–Teller at E → 0

a_op = (3a_t + a_s)/4 + (a_t − a_s)/4 · σ_n·σ_p A_H₂ = κ [ 2a_c + (a_t − a_s)/2 · σ_n·S ] σ_para = 4π κ² (2a_c)² σ_ortho = 4π κ² [ (2a_c)² + (a_t − a_s)²/2 ]

Here a_c = (3a_t + a_s)/4 and κ is the reduced-mass factor. For para-H₂ the spin term vanishes and only the small coherent length a_c survives, because 3a_t ≈ −a_s. Ortho-H₂ keeps the large spin-dependent part, which also drives ortho→para conversion.

Valid for neutron energies well below the 14.7 meV para→ortho rotational threshold. Thermal-energy work needs molecular form factors and rotational transitions.

What the lab solves

The two-body problem reduces to one particle of reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂). With ψ = u_ℓ(r)/r · Y_ℓm the radial equation is

−(ħ²/2μ) u″(r) + [ V(r) + ħ²ℓ(ℓ+1)/(2μr²) + Z₁Z₂e²/r ] u(r) = E u(r), u(0) = 0

For the deuteron the tensor force S₁₂ couples ³S₁ (u) and ³D₁ (w):

−(ħ²/2μ) u″ + V_C u + √8 V_T w = E u −(ħ²/2μ) w″ + [V_C − 2V_T − 3V_LS + 6ħ²/(2μr²)] w + √8 V_T u = E w

Bound states

The radial operator is discretised on r_i = i·h with three-point differences inside a box of radius r_max, giving a symmetric tridiagonal matrix (block tridiagonal with 2×2 blocks for ³S₁–³D₁). A Sturm-sequence count, generalised to blocks through the LDLᵀ inertia, gives the number of eigenvalues below any trial energy, so bisection finds every bound state without missing one. Energies at h and 2h are combined by Richardson extrapolation, E ≈ (4E_h − E_2h)/3, and eigenvectors come from inverse iteration.

Scattering

Numerov integration outward from the origin, matched beyond the nuclear range to Riccati–Bessel functions (neutral) or Coulomb functions F_ℓ, G_ℓ (charged), at two radii:

u(r) → F_ℓ(kr) cos δ_ℓ + G_ℓ(kr) sin δ_ℓ

For pp the Coulomb functions come from their asymptotic expansion at large kr; the nuclear phase is the full phase minus the pure-Coulomb phase computed on the same grid. In ³S₁–³D₁ the code builds the 2×2 K-matrix, diagonalises it into Blatt–Biedenharn eigenphases, and converts them to the Stapp (bar) phases δ̄₁, δ̄₂, ε̄₁ that partial-wave analyses quote.

Low energy

k cot δ₀ = −1/a + ½ r₀ k² + … (neutral) C²(η) k cot δ₀ + (2/a_B) h(η) = −1/a_pp + ½ r₀ k² + … (pp)

For neutral S-waves a and r₀ come exactly from the zero-energy solution; otherwise they are fitted from low-energy phase shifts. The ERE pole k = iκ with κ = [1 − √(1 − 2r₀/a)]/r₀ is a bound state for κ > 0 and a virtual state for κ < 0, as in the np singlet and nn systems.

Glossary

  • Bound state: a solution with E < 0 whose wavefunction vanishes at large r.
  • Scattering length a: the zero-energy limit of scattering, σ(0) = 4πa². a > 0 with a bound state nearby; large negative a means the force almost binds.
  • Effective range r₀: measures the range of the force as seen in low-energy scattering.
  • Phase shift δ_ℓ: how much the potential shifts the outgoing wave in partial wave ℓ. Attraction gives δ > 0, repulsion δ < 0.
  • Virtual state: a pole of the scattering amplitude just above threshold on the unphysical sheet; not a real state, but it makes low-energy scattering very strong.
  • Tensor force: a force that depends on the angle between the spins and the line joining the nucleons; it mixes S and D waves.
  • λ_c: the factor by which the nuclear potential must be multiplied to just produce a bound state.

Exercises for students

  • Fit a square well to the deuteron at R = 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 fm. How do r_d and the effective range change? Compare with the measured r₀ = 1.75 fm.
  • With Reid ³S₁, set the tensor strength to 0. Is the deuteron still bound? Use the parameter scan on the tensor factor to find where binding starts.
  • Find λ_c for the pp and nn systems. How much stronger would the nuclear force need to be for a bound diproton or dineutron?
  • Check Levinson's theorem: start δ₀ for the deuteron channel and count the bound states.
  • Flip the sign of a_s in the H₂ tab. Why does the para cross section rise so sharply?
